How Australia Plays

We're told we're a sporting nation. But behind the grand finals and the gold medals, how do everyday Australians actually move: who plays, where, and does it last a lifetime? The honest answer looks nothing like the highlight reel.

By Timothy Lucas Tong · May 2026 · Data: AusPlay (Australian Sports Commission) & the Australian Bureau of Statistics

Scroll to explore ↓

Ask an Australian about sport and they will picture the Melbourne Cricket Ground on grand final day. Yet the way the country really moves is quieter and more surprising: far more of us are doing laps at the local pool or walking the dog than running onto an oval. Over four short chapters, this story follows the true shape of Australian sport: what we play, where the active millions live, who gets left on the sidelines, and why the hardest person to get moving is a busy 45-year-old. Every figure is real survey data; no statistics background needed.

01

How active are we, really?

Almost everyone moves a little. Far fewer move enough, and when they do, it is rarely at a club. The numbers behind "the sporting nation" tell a more honest story.

Getting started is easy. Keeping it up isn't.

Watch the crowd thin out. Five in six adults move at least once a year, but barely half keep it going three times a week. Australia's real challenge isn't getting people off the couch; it's keeping them off it.

The national sport isn't a contact sport

Australia's number-one sport is one you can do alone, indoors, at any age: swimming pulls in more adults than soccer, tennis and basketball combined. The activities that win are the easy-to-reach ones, not the famous codes that fill stadiums on the weekend.

And most of it happens far from a club

A jog after work, a weekend hike, a hit at the local courts: two in three active adults organise it themselves or with friends. Clubs and gyms still matter, but the real backbone of active Australia is informal and self-directed. (Settings overlap; a person can use several.)

02

Where the active millions are

If most Australians are quietly active on their own, where are they all? Pairing each state's population with the national activity rate maps where the active millions live, and shows why big numbers can fool you.

The active headcount follows the crowd

Sizing each state by its estimated weekly-active people (population scaled by AusPlay's 66% activity rate) puts roughly 5.6 million in New South Wales against 168,000 in the Northern Territory. Bigger isn't fitter, though; it's just bigger.

Half the country lives in two states

The reason is sheer population: New South Wales and Victoria together hold more than half of all Australians. A national tally of "active people" is really a story about where we live, so to compare places fairly, you have to ask how much each person gets. That is where Victoria comes in.

03

Victoria up close: access isn't equal

Down at council level, a fairer test emerges: not how many facilities a place has, but how many it has per person. The answer turns the city-versus-country cliché on its head.

Counter-intuitively, the bush wins

Rural Mallee & Wimmera shires
lead the state per person

Mapped as facilities per 1,000 people, tiny rural shires (Buloke, West Wimmera and Yarriambiack) top the state, while booming outer-metro councils like Melton, Wyndham and Casey sit near the bottom. Rapid growth has outrun the courts, ovals and pools meant to serve it.

Wealth doesn't buy access; if anything, the reverse

Each council is a bubble sized by population, plotting advantage (SEIFA, a standard score where a low value means more disadvantage) against facilities per person. The trend line slopes gently downward: wealthy, dense inner-Melbourne councils have fewer facilities per person, while smaller regional councils have more.

Most councils sit at the low end

Every dot is one council, placed by facilities per 1,000 people and sized by population. The bulk cluster at one to two per 1,000, with a long tail of rural shires reaching past ten. Crucially, the dots on that tail are the smallest: high per-person access is really a story about places with very few residents.

04

Who keeps moving through life?

Staying active isn't only about where you live - it's about where you are in life. Meeting the activity guidelines (enough exercise for real health benefits) rises and falls with age, and tilts with gender and income. Men hold a slim edge over women - about one in four versus one in five - and the genders favour different activities: women lead in Pilates, yoga and walking, men in basketball, soccer and golf.

Fitness has a midlife slump

The share meeting the guidelines slides from 31% of 18–24s to just 16% at 55–64 (the time-poor years of careers and kids), then rebounds sharply to 33% past 65, when people finally have time to walk, swim and garden. The hardest person to get moving isn't the retiree; it's the busy 45-year-old.

Almost nobody lifts

Each square is one adult in 100; green = does the recommended strength activity.

Even among the active, one part of the guidelines is widely skipped: 73 in 100 adults do no muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days a week. Australians are happy to move, far less keen to build strength.

Sport still tracks the wallet

Participation climbs from 79% in lower-income households to 94% in the wealthiest: proof that money and time are still quiet gatekeepers to an active life.

So, are we a sporting nation?

Yes, but not the one on television.

Australians are at their most active in the pool, on the footpath and on their own schedule, and the biggest crowds follow the easiest activities, not the famous codes. Where we fall short is access and staying power: facilities thin out in the fast-growing suburbs, motivation dips in busy midlife, and cost still keeps lower-income households on the sidelines. A genuinely sporting nation is built less on stadiums than on the local pool, path and court, all within reach of everyone, at every age.

About this visualisation

Why. To help an everyday Australian understand how the nation really plays sport, turning raw participation tables into a picture of who is active, where, and how it changes with age and income.

Who. A general Australian audience. Technical terms (SEIFA, "meeting the guidelines") are introduced in plain language where they appear.

What & How. Eleven Vega-Lite visualisations spanning distinct idioms (funnel, lollipop, bar, proportional-symbol map, donut, bubble scatter, choropleth map, beeswarm, line, waffle and dumbbell) and combining two data sources. The custom-built, harder-to-automate pieces include the estimated weekly-active population (population times the national activity rate), the centred-bar participation funnel, the population-sized bubble scatter and beeswarm, and the per-capita facility choropleth.
