How Australia Plays

Australians love their sport — but who actually plays, where do they play it, and does staying active hold up across a lifetime? This is a data story about the everyday reality of sport and physical activity across the country.

By tluct404 · May 2026 · Data: AusPlay (Australian Sports Commission) & the Australian Bureau of Statistics

Scroll to explore ↓

Sport shapes how Australians spend their time, money and weekends — from a Saturday swim to a local footy match. Yet the headline "we are a sporting nation" hides a more interesting picture: participation is uneven across activities, places, ages and incomes. This page walks an everyday reader through that picture in four short chapters, using real national survey data combined with population statistics. No statistics background is needed — every chart is annotated with the point it makes.

01

How active are we, really?

Most Australians move at least sometimes — but the share drops sharply once we ask how often, and most of that activity happens informally rather than at a club.

Activity thins out as we ask for more commitment

Five in six adults are active at least once a year, but only about half manage three or more sessions a week. The gap between "occasionally active" and "regularly active" is where most of the public-health challenge sits.

Swimming is the runaway national sport

Swimming attracts more than three million adult participants — far ahead of soccer, tennis and basketball. The most popular activities are accessible and individual, not the professional team codes we watch on television.

Most of us play on our own terms

Two-thirds of active adults organise activity themselves or with friends. Clubs and gyms matter, but informal, self-directed activity is the backbone of how Australia stays active. (Settings overlap — a person can use several.)

02

Where the active millions are

Combining each state's population with the national weekly-activity rate shows where Australia's active people are concentrated — and why raw numbers can mislead.

Population is heavily skewed to the south-east

New South Wales and Victoria alone hold well over half of all Australians. Any national count of "active people" will be dominated by these two states simply because that is where most people live.

So are the active millions — by sheer headcount

Applying AusPlay's 66% weekly-active rate to each state's population estimates roughly 5.6 million weekly-active people in NSW versus 168,000 in the NT. Big totals reflect big populations — which is exactly why access is better judged per person.

Explore it yourself: click a state to compare

The map and ranking are linked. Click any state — on the map or the bar — to highlight it in both views, and click empty space to reset. It makes the population-driven ordering of "active headcount" tangible, and sets up the real question: is being active equally accessible everywhere? For that we zoom into Victoria.

03

Victoria up close: access isn't equal

Zooming from the nation to Victoria's 79 local councils reveals that the places with the most people are not the places with the best access to sport facilities.

Rural councils are best supplied per person

Measured as facilities per 1,000 residents, small rural shires such as Buloke, West Wimmera and Yarriambiack top the state, while fast-growing outer-metro councils like Melton, Wyndham and Casey sit near the bottom. Big populations dilute per-person access.

Does provision follow community need?

Each circle is a council, sized by its total facilities and coloured by socio-economic advantage (SEIFA). Use the legend to filter deciles and see whether facilities cluster in advantaged or disadvantaged areas. Renders once SEIFA decile and coordinates are added to data/vic_lga.json.

Advantage vs. access, council by council

04

Who keeps moving through life?

Meeting the physical activity guidelines — enough exercise for real health benefits — depends a lot on age, sex and income.

A midlife dip, then a retirement rebound

The share of adults meeting the guidelines falls steadily from the late teens to a low around 55–64, then jumps back up at 65+, when many Australians find time to walk, swim and garden. The challenge years are the busy, time-poor middle decades.

Men edge out women on the guidelines

About one in four men meet the guidelines, compared with one in five women. Preferences differ too: AusPlay finds women lead in Pilates, yoga and walking, while men lead in basketball, soccer and golf.

Sport still tracks the wallet

Participation climbs from 79% in lower-income households to 94% in the highest-earning ones — a reminder that cost and time remain real barriers to an active life.

So what?

Australia is active, but unevenly so. The activities that draw the biggest crowds are the accessible ones; the active headcount follows population, but per-person access does not; and staying active is hardest in busy midlife and in lower-income households. If the goal is a genuinely sporting nation, the data points less at elite codes and more at everyday access — local pools, paths and courts, close to where people actually live.

About this visualisation

Why. To help an everyday Australian understand how the nation really plays sport — turning raw participation tables into a picture of who is active, where, and how it changes with age and income.

Who. A general Australian audience. Technical terms (SEIFA, "meeting the guidelines") are introduced in plain language where they appear.

What & How. Twelve Vega-Lite visualisations — including five maps — combining two data sources. Custom, derived elements include the estimated weekly-active population (population × national activity rate), the linked map-and-bar, the per-capita facility maps and the advantage-vs-access scatter.
